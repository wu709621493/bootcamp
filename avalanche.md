# Avalanche

Avalanches are rapid flows of snow, ice, and debris down a slope, triggered when the snowpack loses cohesion and gravity overwhelms friction. While they can appear sudden, most events are the consequence of slow changes in weather, terrain, and human activity.

## Key Drivers
- **Snowpack structure:** Weak layers, such as faceted crystals or surface hoar, act as sliding planes when overloaded.
- **Weather:** Rapid loading from snowfall, rain-on-snow events, or wind transport increases stress faster than the snowpack can adjust.
- **Terrain:** Slopes between 30° and 45° are most prone; convexities, gullies, and unsupported slopes concentrate stress.
- **Triggers:** Human activity, cornice falls, rockfall, or seismic activity can all initiate a fracture that propagates downslope.

## Impacts
- **Human safety:** Avalanches are a leading cause of winter backcountry fatalities, with burial, trauma, and hypothermia as primary risks.
- **Infrastructure:** Roads, railways, and transmission lines require engineered defenses such as snow sheds, diversion dams, and remote avalanche control.
- **Ecosystems:** Avalanches clear vegetation corridors that can become unique habitats while also redistributing nutrients downslope.

## Risk Reduction
- **Forecasting:** Public avalanche centers combine field observations, stability tests, and weather models to publish daily danger ratings.
- **Travel techniques:** Spacing out, using safe islands of support, and communicating via radios reduce exposure during backcountry travel.
- **Equipment:** Transceivers, probes, shovels, and airbags improve survival odds when an incident occurs, but do not replace prudent terrain choices.
- **Engineering:** Snow fences, starting-zone nets, and controlled releases (explosive or gas-based) help manage hazard above critical infrastructure.

## Indicators of Instability
- Recent avalanches on similar aspects and elevations.
- Whumpfing sounds or shooting cracks while traveling on snow.
- Rapid warming, rain on snow, or heavy snowfall (>2 cm/hour) within the past 24 hours.

## If Caught
1. Attempt to move to the side of the slide or grab a tree or rock outcrop for anchoring.
2. Deploy an airbag if equipped, and discard poles with straps to avoid entanglement.
3. While moving, swim motions help stay near the surface; create an air pocket near the face as the slide slows.
4. After stopping, remain calm to conserve oxygen and wait for rescue; self-extraction is rare once buried.

Understanding avalanche mechanics and applying conservative decision-making are the best defenses against this fast-moving natural hazard.
