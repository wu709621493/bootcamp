# Sunlight Income Index

The **Sunlight Income Index (SII)** is a conceptual metric for estimating how much local prosperity is linked to reliable, useful daylight. It is not a replacement for GDP or household-income statistics; instead, it helps policymakers see where sunlight availability materially shapes productivity, health, and daily costs.

## Why this index can be useful

- **Energy substitution:** More daylight can reduce lighting and heating demand in some climates.
- **Labor productivity:** Outdoor and daylight-sensitive sectors (agriculture, tourism, logistics, construction) often track light windows.
- **Health and education effects:** Daylight exposure can influence sleep quality, mood, and learning performance, which then affects long-run earnings.
- **Urban form equity:** Neighborhoods with poor daylight access may incur hidden costs (higher electricity use, lower comfort, lower retail footfall).

## Proposed structure

One simple approach is a weighted score on a 0–100 scale:

\[
\text{SII} = 100 \times \sum_i w_i z_i
\]

Where each \(z_i\) is a normalized component and \(w_i\) is a policy-chosen weight.

Suggested components:

1. **Daylight Availability Score**
   - Annual effective daylight hours (season-adjusted).
2. **Economic Daylight Utilization Score**
   - Share of local output produced in daylight-sensitive sectors.
3. **Built Environment Access Score**
   - Residential and workplace daylight penetration, obstruction index, and street-canyon shading.
4. **Sunlight-to-Savings Score**
   - Estimated household utility savings attributable to daylight.
5. **Equity Adjustment Score**
   - Distribution of daylight access across income deciles.

## Interpretation

- **High SII:** The region converts natural light into broad-based economic and social benefit.
- **Low SII:** Daylight potential is underused or unequally distributed; reforms in planning, housing design, and service timing may help.

## Practical policy applications

- Prioritize daylight-preserving zoning in dense districts.
- Set minimum daylight standards for affordable housing.
- Align school and clinic schedules with local light profiles.
- Target subsidies where low-income households face high artificial-light dependence.

## Caveats

- The index should be climate-sensitive: intense sun can impose cooling burdens.
- Correlation is not causation; use with quasi-experimental or panel analysis where possible.
- Standardized measurement protocols are required to avoid cross-city bias.

Used carefully, the Sunlight Income Index can add a missing dimension to inclusive-growth planning: not just how much income is generated, but how naturally and equitably local environments support that income.
