# Wither

A desert wind whispered through the balcony garden, tracing dry maps on leaves that once drank monsoon air. The plants did not die in spectacle; they tightened into small, patient questions. In the brittle hours before dawn, I misted them and watched how thin silver beads gathered at the edges, defying the pull of dust.

I wondered what it meant for a habit to wither. An evening practice skipped once becomes a cautionary tale, skipped twice becomes a choice. The third time, the ritual crumbles into sand between routine stones. Yet even sand retains the memory of bedrock, and with gentle pressure it can be fused again.

Withering is not only decay. It is the body's reminder that attention is a currency spent or saved. When roots lose reach, they send signals to extend or retreat. When relationships dry at the margins, we can add water, shade, or simply acknowledge the season. Not everything should return to lushness, but nothing should be ignored as it turns.

I move a pot into softer light. I prune what no longer holds its shape. I refill the watering can and accept that tenderness, too, is a practice that can crack if left in the sun. The leaves do not complain; they only test whether my care arrives before the next wind.
