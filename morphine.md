# Morphine Overview

Morphine is a potent opioid analgesic derived from opium poppies and used to manage moderate to severe acute and chronic pain. It acts primarily on mu-opioid receptors to alter pain perception and response, while also affecting respiratory drive and gastrointestinal motility.

## Mechanism and Pharmacology
- **Receptor activity:** Full agonist at mu-opioid receptors; weaker effects at kappa and delta receptors.
- **Onset and duration:** IV onset in minutes with 3–4 hour duration; oral onset within 30–60 minutes with 4–6 hour duration.
- **Metabolism:** Primarily glucuronidated in the liver to morphine-3-glucuronide (inactive/irritant) and morphine-6-glucuronide (active analgesic). Both metabolites are renally cleared.
- **Variability:** Analgesic response varies with genetics, organ function, concurrent CNS depressants, and opioid tolerance.

## Clinical Uses
- Acute postoperative pain, myocardial infarction pain, and severe traumatic injuries.
- Chronic cancer-related pain and selected cases of palliative care for dyspnea relief.
- Preferred when hemodynamic stability and predictable titration are needed; avoid in uncontrolled respiratory compromise.

## Dosing Principles
- **Start low, titrate carefully:** Initiate with the lowest effective dose and adjust based on pain control and adverse effects.
- **Route selection:** IV for rapid titration in monitored settings; oral immediate-release for intermittent pain; oral extended-release for stable chronic pain in opioid-tolerant patients.
- **Renal and hepatic considerations:** Reduce doses or extend intervals in renal impairment; monitor closely with liver dysfunction.
- **Equianalgesic conversions:** When switching opioids or routes, use equianalgesic charts and reduce calculated dose by 25–50% for incomplete cross-tolerance.

## Safety and Risks
- **Respiratory depression and sedation:** Highest risk during initiation, dose escalation, or when combined with benzodiazepines, alcohol, or other sedatives.
- **Constipation:** Nearly universal; start bowel regimen with stimulant laxative ± stool softener.
- **Nausea, pruritus, and hypotension:** Common early effects; monitor and treat symptomatically.
- **Dependence and addiction:** Prolonged use can cause physical dependence; misuse carries risk of opioid use disorder and overdose.
- **Contraindications:** Significant respiratory depression, acute asthma exacerbation without resuscitative equipment, and paralytic ileus.

## Monitoring and Harm Reduction
- Assess pain relief, sedation level, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation after dose changes.
- Provide naloxone access and education for patients at elevated overdose risk.
- Review prescription monitoring programs and evaluate for substance use history before initiation.
- Counsel patients on safe storage, avoiding sharing medication, and not combining with alcohol or sedatives.

## Tapering and Discontinuation
- Taper gradually (e.g., 5–10% dose reduction every 1–4 weeks) to limit withdrawal symptoms such as anxiety, muscle aches, insomnia, and gastrointestinal distress.
- Slow or pause the taper if severe withdrawal or uncontrolled pain occurs; consider non-opioid adjuncts and behavioral support.

## Alternatives and Adjuncts
- Use multimodal analgesia: acetaminophen, NSAIDs (if safe), neuropathic agents (gabapentinoids, SNRIs, TCAs), regional anesthesia, and physical or cognitive therapies.
- Reserve or escalate to morphine only when benefits outweigh risks and after reviewing non-opioid options.
