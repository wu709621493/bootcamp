# Wake

Waking is a small negotiation with gravity and memory: the body remembers where it ended, the mind renegotiates what still
matters. Morning light audits both, inviting us to keep what heals and release what clings.

## First light inventory
- **Breath check:** slow inhale, slower exhale. Confirm the night did not borrow too much.
- **Body map:** neck, shoulders, hips, feet. Note what tightened, what softened, what asks for water.
- **Temperature glance:** open a window or touch the floor. Decide whether the day begins with warmth or briskness.
- **Signal scan:** phone remains face down for five minutes. Let the room speak first.

## Gentle ignition
1. Pour a small glass of water with a pinch of salt. The tongue wakes the gut, the gut wakes the brain.
2. Stretch until the first yawn. The yawn is the body's handshake with daylight.
3. Step outside, even for thirty seconds. Natural light is a soft reset button for circadian clocks.
4. Choose one intention. Write it down. Let it be specific and kind.

## Avoiding false alarms
- Resist urgency masquerading as importance. Not every notification deserves cortisol.
- If dread appears, give it a task: draft the simplest next move instead of rehearsing disaster.
- Coffee after breakfast prevents blood sugar whiplash; fatigue is not a moral failure, just a signal.

## Staying awake after waking
- Movement every hour keeps vigilance from crystallizing into tension.
- Alternate focus and drift: twenty minutes of effort, two minutes of window-staring.
- Ask one curious question before noon. Curiosity is caffeine without the crash.
- Close the day with gratitude for what stayed awake in you: patience, humor, or just the humble willingness to try again.

Wakefulness is not merely the absence of sleep. It is the practice of greeting the world without flinching, letting light
arrive as information rather than judgment.

## Wake's end
- Lower the lights and lower the volume of the day. Evening is the body's gentle dimmer switch.
- Log one lesson and one kindness. Memory settles better when it has anchors.
- Set the room for tomorrow: water on the nightstand, phone facedown, clothes waiting.
- Give your last breath a name: release, forgiveness, or simple quiet.
