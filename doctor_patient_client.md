# Who is the client in medicine: the doctor or the patient?

In standard clinical ethics and care delivery, **the patient is the primary client**.

## Why

1. **Fiduciary duty runs from clinician to patient.**
   Doctors owe duties of beneficence, non-maleficence, confidentiality, and informed consent to the patient.
2. **Clinical decisions are patient-centered.**
   Diagnosis and treatment are justified by the patient’s interests, values, and goals.
3. **Consent authority is patient-side.**
   The patient (or lawful surrogate) authorizes interventions.

## Important caveat: who pays is not always who is the client

In real systems, physicians and hospitals interact with other stakeholders:
- Insurers and governments (reimbursement and authorization)
- Employers or institutions (occupational or panel arrangements)
- Families/caregivers (support and logistics)

These parties can be **customers, payers, or stakeholders**, but they are generally **not the primary clinical client** in the ethical sense.

## Edge cases

- **Occupational medicine / independent medical exams (IME):**
  The physician may be contracted by an employer/insurer for evaluation rather than treatment. Here, role clarity is essential: this is not a typical therapeutic doctor–patient relationship.
- **Public health roles:**
  Clinicians may carry duties to population safety that can limit individual preference (e.g., mandatory reporting).

## Practical bottom line

If your question is about ordinary treatment care, the answer is:

> **The patient is the client; the doctor is the professional fiduciary serving that client.**
