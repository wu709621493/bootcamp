# Memory Accessibility Strategy Design for People Living With Parkinson’s Disease and Alzheimer’s Disease

## Purpose and audience
This document outlines practical, human-centered strategies to improve memory accessibility for individuals living with Parkinson’s Disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). It is intended for caregivers, clinicians, designers, and program planners who need actionable guidance for daily support, environments, and tools.

## Guiding principles
- **Respect autonomy:** Support choice-making and consent, even when assistance is needed.
- **Reduce cognitive load:** Simplify tasks, environment, and interfaces to lower memory burden.
- **Preserve dignity:** Avoid infantilization; use adult-appropriate language and design.
- **Prioritize safety:** Balance independence with risk reduction in a non-restrictive way.
- **Enable continuity:** Maintain familiar routines, cues, and meaningful relationships.
- **Support motor variability:** PD adds motor challenges; ensure accessibility beyond memory.

## Cognitive and motor profiles to consider
### Alzheimer’s Disease (typical progression)
- Difficulty forming new memories (encoding) and retrieving recent events.
- Disorientation in time and place, language challenges, reduced executive function.
- Progressive decline in independent task sequencing and judgement.

### Parkinson’s Disease (typical progression)
- Slowed thinking (bradyphrenia), attention and working memory challenges.
- Executive dysfunction, visuospatial impairments in some individuals.
- Motor symptoms (tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia) that impede interaction.

### Combined considerations
- Memory cues must be **consistent**, **redundant**, and **multi-sensory**.
- Tools need to support **motor variability** (large buttons, voice input, simplified gestures).
- Fatigue, medication cycles, and “on/off” periods affect access and reliability.

## Strategy framework
### 1) Environment design
**Goal:** Make surroundings self-explanatory and supportive.
- **Visual anchors:** Use large, high-contrast labels on doors, drawers, and rooms (e.g., “Bathroom,” “Clothes”).
- **Pathway clarity:** Remove clutter, improve lighting, and provide consistent wayfinding cues.
- **Memory stations:** Establish fixed places for essentials (keys, phone, glasses, medications).
- **Calendars and clocks:** Use oversized day-date clocks with plain language (“Tuesday morning”).
- **Safety prompts:** Color-coded stove knobs, automatic shutoff devices, and checklists near exits.

### 2) Routine engineering
**Goal:** Reduce dependence on short-term memory through stable structure.
- **Daily scripts:** Consistent morning/evening sequences with printed step cards or pictograms.
- **Time-blocking:** Divide day into predictable segments (meals, meds, rest, movement).
- **Event previews:** Provide a “today card” in the morning; review again before bedtime.
- **Medication coupling:** Tie medication to anchored events (breakfast, after brushing teeth).

### 3) Multimodal memory cues
**Goal:** Reinforce recall using multiple channels.
- **Visual:** Photos, icons, labels, and color-coded objects.
- **Auditory:** Alarms with spoken prompts (“Take morning medication”).
- **Tactile:** Textured tags on drawers or items to distinguish categories.
- **Social:** Caregiver prompts that use consistent phrasing and gentle repetition.

### 4) Assistive tools and technology
**Goal:** Create low-friction memory support that adapts to motor limitations.
- **Simplified devices:** Dedicated reminder devices with large buttons and voice output.
- **Voice assistants:** Short, structured prompts; avoid multi-step instructions.
- **Wearables:** Vibration-based reminders for hearing or attention variability.
- **Medication dispensers:** Locked, scheduled dispensers with audio/visual cues.
- **Emergency access:** One-touch calling with photo-based contacts.

### 5) Communication strategies
**Goal:** Improve comprehension and reduce frustration.
- **One idea at a time:** Use short, concrete sentences.
- **Confirm understanding:** Ask yes/no or forced-choice questions rather than open-ended queries.
- **Validation:** Acknowledge emotions before redirecting to a task.
- **Consistent language:** Use the same words for the same tasks (“let’s do our walk now”).

### 6) Task simplification and scaffolding
**Goal:** Enable success by reducing steps and complexity.
- **Chunking:** Break tasks into 3–5 steps, each with clear completion cues.
- **Visual sequencing:** Step-by-step photo cards for dressing, meals, or hygiene.
- **Progress cues:** Checkboxes or sliding markers to show task progression.
- **Adaptive tools:** Easy-grip utensils, non-slip mats, and button extenders.

### 7) Social and emotional supports
**Goal:** Maintain identity, relationships, and motivation.
- **Life story prompts:** A memory book with photos and short captions.
- **Familiar music:** Personalized playlists for mood regulation and cueing.
- **Meaningful roles:** Assign light responsibilities (watering plants, folding towels).
- **Community connection:** Short, predictable visits and participation in group activities.

### 8) Safety, ethics, and consent
**Goal:** Protect the person while preserving rights.
- **Least restrictive supports:** Prefer cues and prompts over restraints.
- **Privacy considerations:** Use discreet tracking when necessary and agreed upon.
- **Risk assessment:** Evaluate wandering risks, medication errors, and falls.
- **Advance planning:** Document preferences for care, communication, and living arrangements.

## Program implementation checklist
- [ ] Assess cognitive and motor baselines; update quarterly.
- [ ] Map daily routines and identify frequent breakdown points.
- [ ] Standardize environment labeling and storage locations.
- [ ] Implement reminder systems with redundancy (visual + audio).
- [ ] Train caregivers on consistent cues and response strategies.
- [ ] Review medication scheduling and dispense methods with clinicians.
- [ ] Create emergency and escalation protocols.

## Example daily workflow (light support)
**Morning**
- Wake-up alarm with spoken prompt.
- Bathroom sign + step card for hygiene.
- Breakfast station with labeled bins.
- Medication dispenser with confirmation cue.

**Midday**
- Scheduled walk or movement activity.
- Simple lunch with visual meal plan.
- Rest period with calming music.

**Evening**
- “Today review” card + tomorrow preview.
- Nighttime hygiene sequence card.
- Safety check (doors, stove, lights).

## Measurement and iteration
- Track incidents (missed medications, disorientation episodes, falls).
- Measure quality-of-life indicators (engagement, mood, sleep consistency).
- Gather caregiver feedback monthly; adjust cues and routines.
- Reassess tool usability during PD medication “off” times.

## Notes on personalization
Every individual will have unique preferences, cultural norms, and routines. The strategy is most effective when co-designed with the person and their care team, integrating familiar language, objects, and routines.
