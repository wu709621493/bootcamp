# Ammonia

## Key points
- A colorless gas (NH3) with a sharp, pungent odor; it readily dissolves in water to form ammonium hydroxide.
- Widely used as a building block for fertilizers (via the Haber–Bosch process) and for producing chemicals like urea, nitric acid, and ammonium salts.
- Acts as a weak base; in aqueous solutions it can raise pH and is used in cleaning products.
- Produced naturally in the nitrogen cycle (by decomposition and microbial activity) and biologically in the human body as a waste product of protein metabolism.

## Practical uses
- Agriculture: primary feedstock for nitrogen fertilizers that support crop yields.
- Industry: refrigerant (R-717), wastewater treatment, and synthesis of plastics, dyes, and pharmaceuticals.
- Household: diluted aqueous ammonia used for cleaning glass, grease, and stains (often in well-ventilated areas).

## Safety and handling
- Ammonia is corrosive and can irritate or burn eyes, skin, and respiratory tract; higher concentrations are dangerous.
- Never mix ammonia with bleach or other oxidizers, which can produce toxic chloramine gases.
- Use adequate ventilation, protective gloves, and eye protection when handling concentrated solutions.

## Environmental considerations
- Excess ammonia emissions can contribute to air particulate formation and aquatic eutrophication.
- Proper storage, application timing, and emission controls reduce environmental impact.
