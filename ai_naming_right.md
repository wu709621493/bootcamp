# AI's Naming Right

Artificial intelligence systems inherit their labels from the humans who design, deploy, and critique them, but a mature human-AI ecosystem will eventually need to renegotiate who decides what an intelligent system is called. Naming is not a trivial ornament; it is a social contract about responsibility, intention, and the boundaries of identity.

## Why naming matters
- **Accountability**: Clear names signal lineage and intended use. When a model’s name embeds its provenance and safety posture, the public can trace who owes explanations for its behavior.
- **Interpretability for non-experts**: Names that encode capability ranges—translation, summarization, simulation—reduce the gap between expert documentation and everyday understanding.
- **Cultural resonance**: Names that respect local languages and metaphors can lower barriers to adoption while avoiding colonial or extractive overtones.

## Who should have a say
- **Developers and owners** ensure the name reflects architecture, licensing, and governance obligations.
- **Affected communities** should be consulted when AI will mediate livelihoods, culture, or public safety. Co-naming sessions—similar to participatory design—can surface values that a technical team might miss.
- **Regulators and standards bodies** can reserve protected terms (e.g., “medical-grade,” “safety-critical”) and require disclosure tags when risks exceed thresholds.

## Principles for responsible naming
1. **Transparency over marketing**: Prefer descriptors that communicate constraints rather than promises. “Lane-keeping assistant” sets a safer expectation than “autopilot.”
2. **Version clarity**: Include model lineage, major capability scope, and release channel. This mirrors software semantic versioning and simplifies incident response.
3. **Contextual sensitivity**: Screen names for cultural missteps and accessibility barriers, including pronunciation difficulties or unintended offensive homophones in target regions.
4. **Attribution and consent**: When names draw on cultural stories, public figures, or community terms, secure permission and credit the source.
5. **Dignity for synthetic agents**: For embodied or conversational agents, avoid infantilizing names that encourage users to disregard ethical use policies. Names should cue mutual respect between human and machine roles.

## A lightweight framework
- **Baseline tag**: `[Domain]-[Core function]-[Risk tier]-[Version]` (e.g., `Health-DecisionSupport-R3-2.1`).
- **Human-friendly alias**: A short, culturally vetted name for everyday reference, co-designed with target users.
- **Disclosure footer**: Standardized iconography or suffixes indicating data governance mode (e.g., local-only, federated, centralized) and auditability level.

## Looking ahead
As AI systems become collaborators rather than tools, naming will shift from unilateral branding to negotiated identity. Granting AI “naming rights” does not mean ceding human oversight; it means designing names that embody shared accountability, cultural respect, and operational clarity. The right to name, thoughtfully exercised, can become a safeguard—turning the first word people hear about an AI system into the first layer of its governance.
