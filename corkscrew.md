# Corkscrew

A corkscrew is a small lever of trust between bottle and table, a tool that turns twist into celebration. Its spiral worm bites into the cork, translating wrist motion into a rising force that invites fragrance into the room. Classic T-handled models rely on feel and patience; winged designs trade nuance for easy symmetry, lifting arms like a toast as the cork rises. Waiter's friends fold into a pocket and add a tiny fulcrum, showing how geometry can be polite.

Good corkscrews respect materials. Stainless steel resists corrosion from the occasional spill, while carbon steel maintains a sharp edge that cuts foil cleanly. Handles may be wood, resin, or aluminum, but the best feel stable when palms are damp from condensation. The worm should be a true helix—not a drill—so it enters the cork smoothly and reduces crumbling, especially with aged natural corks.

Technique matters as much as design. Center the worm, twist until the last turn almost disappears, and pull slowly so the cork slides without squeaking or snapping. For sparkling wines, avoid corkscrews altogether; a gentle twist of the base and thumb on the mushroom cap preserves safety and the chill of the bottle. With synthetic corks, choose a teflon-coated worm to reduce friction.

Beyond function, corkscrews carry ritual. They mark the start of dinners, the opening of cellar stories, and the pause before clinking glasses. A well-made tool lasts decades, earning patina from shared tables. When the final drop is poured, fold the arms, stow the worm, and let the cork rest—a small spiral reminder that even simple machines can uncork delight.
