# Review: Technologies Used to Manipulate Energy

## Scope
This review covers practical technologies that **convert, store, transport, control, and optimize** energy across major domains: electrical, thermal, mechanical, chemical, and electromagnetic.

## 1) Conversion Technologies

### Power generation and conversion
- **Turbomachinery** (steam turbines, gas turbines, hydro turbines): converts fluid/thermal potential into rotational mechanical energy, then electricity via generators.
- **Internal combustion engines**: converts chemical energy in fuel to mechanical work (transport, backup generation).
- **Fuel cells** (PEM, SOFC): electrochemical conversion of hydrogen or hydrocarbons to electricity with high efficiency and low local emissions.
- **Photovoltaics (PV)**: direct conversion of photons to electricity via semiconductor junctions.
- **Thermoelectric generators**: converts temperature gradients to electricity (waste heat recovery, remote sensors).

## 2) Storage Technologies

### Electrochemical and chemical storage
- **Lithium-ion batteries** (LFP, NMC): dominant for mobile electronics, EVs, and grid balancing due to high energy density and controllability.
- **Flow batteries** (vanadium, zinc-bromine): decoupled energy/power scaling suitable for long-duration stationary storage.
- **Hydrogen systems**: surplus electricity converted to hydrogen (electrolysis), then used in fuel cells/turbines or industrial feedstock.

### Mechanical and thermal storage
- **Pumped hydro storage**: stores electrical energy as gravitational potential; mature, large-scale grid balancing.
- **Compressed air energy storage (CAES)**: stores energy by pressurizing air; later expanded through turbines.
- **Flywheels**: high-power, short-duration storage for frequency regulation.
- **Thermal energy storage** (molten salt, phase-change materials): stores heat/cold for power systems and building HVAC shifting.

## 3) Transmission and Distribution

- **High-voltage AC/DC grids**: bulk energy transfer over distance; HVDC is especially useful for long-distance and subsea links.
- **Power electronics interfaces** (inverters, rectifiers, converters): enable integration of renewables, batteries, and variable-speed drives.
- **Transformers and switchgear**: voltage control, protection, and safe routing of electrical energy.
- **District heating/cooling networks**: thermal energy transport using centralized generation and distributed exchange.

## 4) End-Use Manipulation and Control

### Industrial and building systems
- **Variable frequency drives (VFDs)**: dynamically control motor speed/torque, reducing wasted electrical energy.
- **Heat pumps**: move heat rather than generate it directly, often delivering 2–5x thermal output per unit electric input.
- **Combined heat and power (CHP)**: captures waste heat from generation for process heat or building heating.
- **Advanced insulation and heat exchangers**: improve thermal management and reduce conversion losses.

### Smart control and digital optimization
- **SCADA/EMS/BMS** platforms: monitor and optimize generation, storage, and loads.
- **Demand response systems**: shift consumption in time to match supply and reduce peak demand.
- **AI/ML forecasting**: improves dispatch of renewables, storage, and flexible loads.
- **Digital twins and predictive maintenance**: minimize downtime and improve lifecycle efficiency.

## 5) Emerging and Frontier Technologies

- **Solid-state batteries**: potential improvements in safety and energy density.
- **Perovskite solar cells**: high efficiency potential with low-cost manufacturing pathways.
- **Small modular reactors (SMRs)**: compact nuclear options for firm low-carbon power.
- **Superconducting devices**: low-loss transmission and high-performance magnetic systems (still deployment-limited).
- **Power-to-X pathways**: converts electricity into synthetic fuels or chemicals for hard-to-abate sectors.

## 6) Evaluation Criteria

When reviewing energy-manipulation technologies, key decision criteria include:
- **Efficiency** (first-law and system-level)
- **Response speed** and controllability
- **Power density vs. energy density**
- **Capital cost and lifecycle cost**
- **Safety and reliability**
- **Material availability and supply chain risk**
- **Environmental impact** (emissions, land/water use, recyclability)
- **Scalability and grid/market compatibility**

## Conclusion
Energy manipulation is increasingly a **systems integration challenge** rather than a single-device challenge. The strongest real-world outcomes typically combine:
1. high-efficiency conversion,
2. right-duration storage,
3. power-electronics-rich control,
4. software-driven optimization,
5. and domain-specific infrastructure constraints.
