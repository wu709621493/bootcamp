# Science Romance Fiction

Dr. Mira Haldane kept her wedding ring in a gravity well.

That is, she left it in a small titanium cup bolted to the floor of the centrifuge at the edge of Station Thistle. The ring held against the radial force like a tiny planet against the pull of its star, and every time she stepped into the chamber, it glittered at her feet and reminded her of all the orbits she had left behind.

She told herself she was practical. Rings snagged on glove seals. Rings conducted heat. Rings carried meaning that could get in the way of her work. The work was simple in principle: to coax a ribbon of alien ice to speak about its origin. The comet fragment, recently captured in a slow flyby, was older than the star itself, a drifted archive sealed in transparent latitudes of frozen gases.

Yet the practical decision had become a ritual. The ring went into its gravity well and Mira went to the lab where the centrifuge spun and the physics hummed like a hymn.

On the day of the anomaly, the station was quiet in a way that the silence became another instrument. The spectrometer arms crept through their routine, and the ice core emitted a faint, rhythmic pulse of light.

"That's not in the models," said Leo Arias, her partner in the lab, but not, she had told herself, in life. His voice had always carried a warmth that made the air feel breathable.

"No," she said, and then let the data wash over her. The light flickered as though the sample was blinking.

"Could be trapped plasma," he offered.

"Or a message," she said before she could stop herself.

He laughed softly. "I don't believe in cosmic love letters."

"I don't, either," she lied.

They sat in the glow of the monitors, two human beings orbiting a point of curiosity, and in the glass of the lab partition Mira saw her own reflection and Leo's side by side. There was a crooked line of shadow between them, like a boundary.

The pulse repeated every 143 seconds. The number lodged in her mind like a seed. It took a full night of computation before she recognized it: the spin period of Thistle itself. The fragment was resonating with the station's rotation.

"It's coupling to our spin," she said, and the words were a relief. "We can map it. We can see the internal structure through phase response."

"We can hear it," he corrected. He piped the signal through a conversion, and the lab filled with a low, rolling thrum that rose and fell. It was the same frequency as the station's artificial gravity, as the rotation that kept her ring anchored in its cup.

She felt her chest answer with its own rhythm.

Over the next few days, they leaned closer together, trading the mirror distance for the real thing. Their hands crossed over consoles, their breath caught on the same jokes, their shoulders brushed with the subtle gravity of the station's spin.

"You always talk like there's a center," Leo said one night as they watched the oscillation trace a clean sine wave. "But everything we do is about avoiding the center."

"You mean the centrifuge?"

"I mean the heartbreak," he said.

Mira looked at the ring in its cup, a gold band that had never once failed to fit her finger. It was easy to believe in orbits, harder to believe in landing.

"We can use the oscillation to generate a map," she said instead, and he let her change the subject.

They could see the structure soon after, when the data painted a layered interior of hollow pockets, a lattice of frozen channels like the negative space of a giant snowflake. It was beautiful. It was precise. It was empty.

Until they found a bubble of warmth, a small cavity that refused to freeze, tucked within the core. There was a seam around it, a dimpled edge that looked engineered.

"Could be a jet pocket," Leo said.

"Or a capsule," Mira answered.

She was the one who volunteered to go in the cold suit. She justified it by seniority, by the way the gloves fit her hands just right. The truth was she wanted to be the one to touch something older than stars, to be the one to bring back a story.

She moved through the tunnel, a dark tube lined with shimmering frost, the sound of her breath a metronome. The capsule was small, the size of a fist, and it glowed faintly. She held it against the light and saw that it wasn't metal or rock. It was glass.

There was a lock. It opened when she rested her palm against it, as though the thing could read her warmth. Inside sat a thin coil of filament and a slip of paper sealed against the eons.

She read the inscription once, then again, and then kept reading as though the words might change.

*If you are warm, you are living. If you are living, you are loved. If you are loved, you are part of me.*

There was no signature, no date, no coordinates. The paper smelled of old ozone. The filament pulsed once when she held it, a heartbeat from the deep.

Mira returned to the lab with the capsule cradled in her arms. Leo looked up, and the glow from the thing washed across his face. For a moment, she imagined them both as artifacts, sealed in a corridor of time.

"It's a message," she said.

"A love letter," he replied.

She wanted to laugh and cry at once. The station spun around them, and the ring in its gravity well continued to glitter. The cosmos had always been indifferent, she told herself, but this was not indifference. This was intention. Somewhere, someone had sent a warmth across an age of ice, trusting that someone else would be there to receive it.

She had not known how hungry she was for that trust.

That night, when she stepped into the centrifuge, she did not leave the ring in its cup. She slid it on, feeling the familiar circle fit her finger like a promise. She found Leo at the lab door, and he held her palm, tracing the imprint of the capsule's lock on her skin.

"Do you believe in cosmic love letters now?" she asked.

He brushed his thumb over her knuckles. "Only if we write one back."

They drafted a reply together, a string of light encoded with the station's spin, with the pulse of their hearts, with the simple physics of their presence. When they released it into the comet's hollow, it shone once and then vanished into the cold.

Mira watched the signal go and felt herself, at last, fall into a new orbit.
