# Supreme Court Trial on Sushi

At dawn the marble columns held their breath,
while chopsticks clicked like gavels in the hall.
Nine robes convened to weigh a grain of rice,
and asked if taste belongs to one—or all.

"Exhibit A," the clerk declared, "bluefin,
seared once by sun, then folded into law."
The plaintiff bowed: "This nigiri was mine,
until the defense consumed it, teeth and awe."

The defense rose softly: "Hunger is a right.
The Constitution does not mention soy,
but liberty includes the wasabi sting,
and joy should not be taxed by claims of joy."

A justice frowned: "What precedent have we?"
"Miranda v. Miso," counsel answered, dry.
"And Roe v. Roe," a whisper from the crowd,
"which held that fish may choose the sea—or fry."

Outside, reporters sniffed the legal air,
their notebooks salted by a drifting breeze.
Inside, the courtroom tasted of the tide,
of old debates and newly opened seas.

The Chief leaned in. "We cannot split this roll.
Yet law, like rice, is pressed by patient hands.
No one owns wonder once it's on the tongue;
we share what nourishes across our lands."

So ruled the Court: one platter, many claims,
and none so pure they stand without the rest.
They struck the gavel. Soy in crystal cups
was poured for all. Even dissent was blessed.

By noon the steps were crowded, bright, and loud.
A child held up a menu like a sign:
**Equal protection under sesame.**
The crowd cheered back, "And extra pickled brine!"
