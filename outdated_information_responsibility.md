# Outdated Information Responsibility

Information ages the moment it is published. Facts shift, context widens, and interpretations change. Owning responsibility for outdated information prevents confusion and keeps knowledge ecosystems healthy.

## Why responsibility matters
- **Trust maintenance:** People rely on sources to keep details current; when data lags, trust erodes faster than it is earned.
- **Safety implications:** Inaccurate medical, legal, or safety guidance can cause harm if left uncorrected.
- **Reputational continuity:** Authors and institutions protect their credibility by proactively flagging what is stale.
- **Collective learning:** Clear update practices help others build on accurate foundations instead of compounding mistakes.

## Roles and duties
- **Authors and publishers**
  - Provide publication dates and version identifiers for every release.
  - Include a short "last reviewed" note with upcoming review dates.
  - Maintain changelogs that summarize what has changed and what remains uncertain.
- **Maintainers and platform owners**
  - Automate alerts for content approaching review windows.
  - Archive superseded versions and redirect readers to current material.
  - Offer structured feedback channels for readers to report outdated claims.
- **Readers and downstream users**
  - Check dates, sources, and version tags before citing or implementing guidance.
  - Report contradictions or obsolete references with specific examples and links.
  - Avoid spreading content known to be stale without clarifying its status.

## Practices to limit harm
- Treat time-stamped assumptions as provisional and replace them with measured intervals when possible (e.g., "data current through Q1 2024").
- Embed uncertainty statements that age well, such as the conditions under which a conclusion would change.
- Use structured metadata (JSON-LD, schema.org) to expose update cycles to search and monitoring tools.
- When retiring content, leave a visible notice explaining why and where to find current guidance.

## Transparency checklist for updates
- Does the page clearly show when it was published and last reviewed?
- Are links to newer sources or errata provided?
- Is there an accessible history of revisions for auditors and researchers?
- Are potential impacts of outdated sections identified (safety, compliance, financial)?
- Does the governance model assign named stewards to review the material on a schedule?

Owning outdated information is a continuous task: signal freshness, invite correction, and make it easy for everyone to navigate toward the most current truth.
