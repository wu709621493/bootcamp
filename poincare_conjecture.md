# Poincaré Conjecture

The Poincaré conjecture asks whether every simply connected, closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere \(S^3\). Posed by Henri Poincaré in 1904, it became a central question in topology because it characterizes the 3-sphere using intrinsic properties.

## Statement

- A **3-manifold** is a space that locally looks like \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
- **Closed** means compact and without boundary.
- **Simply connected** means every loop can be continuously contracted to a point.

The conjecture claims that any 3-manifold with these properties must be topologically the same as \(S^3\).

## Key Ideas in the Resolution

- **Ricci flow with surgery:** Richard Hamilton introduced Ricci flow, an evolution equation that smooths the metric of a manifold. Singularities can develop, so the flow is periodically modified by "surgery" to remove them while tracking topological change.
- **Geometrization Program:** William Thurston conjectured that every compact 3-manifold decomposes into pieces with uniform geometric structures. The Poincaré conjecture is a special case where the only possible geometry is spherical.
- **Perelman's breakthrough:** Grigori Perelman (2002–2003) provided analytic tools, including entropy formulas and the no-local-collapsing theorem, to control Ricci flow with surgery and complete Hamilton's program.

## Proof Status and Impact

Perelman's work was verified by the community and published in detailed expositions by Cao–Zhu and Kleiner–Lott. The Clay Mathematics Institute recognized the proof as resolving one of its Millennium Problems, offering a \$1 million prize, which Perelman declined. The result confirms a full classification of simply connected 3-manifolds and cements Ricci flow as a powerful technique in geometric analysis.
