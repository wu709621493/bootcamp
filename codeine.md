# Codeine Overview

Codeine is a naturally derived opioid used for mild to moderate pain and cough suppression. It is a prodrug that relies on hepatic metabolism to morphine for much of its analgesic effect, so patient genetics and drug interactions markedly influence efficacy and safety.

## Mechanism and Pharmacology
- **Receptor activity:** Weak mu-opioid receptor agonist; primary analgesia occurs after O-demethylation to morphine via CYP2D6.
- **Onset and duration:** Oral onset typically within 30–60 minutes; analgesia lasts about 4–6 hours.
- **Metabolism and elimination:** CYP2D6 converts ~5–10% of a dose to morphine; CYP3A4 converts a portion to inactive norcodeine. Metabolites are glucuronidated and renally cleared.
- **Genetic variability:** Poor metabolizers may get little benefit; ultra-rapid metabolizers can generate high morphine levels and are at elevated overdose risk.

## Clinical Uses
- Short-term treatment of mild to moderate nociceptive pain when non-opioids are insufficient.
- Antitussive in select situations, though many guidelines now prefer non-opioid alternatives.
- Often formulated with acetaminophen or NSAIDs to enhance analgesia; dosing must account for all ingredients.

## Dosing Principles
- **Start low and reassess:** Typical adult oral doses are 15–60 mg every 4–6 hours as needed, not exceeding recommended daily limits or accompanying acetaminophen ceilings.
- **Avoid in rapid or poor metabolizers:** Genetic testing or clinical history (e.g., prior high sensitivity or non-response) should inform use; avoid in known ultra-rapid metabolizers.
- **Renal and hepatic impairment:** Reduce doses or extend intervals; monitor closely for sedation and respiratory depression.
- **Pediatric cautions:** Contraindicated after tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy and generally avoided in children under 12; use extreme caution in adolescents with sleep-disordered breathing.

## Safety and Risks
- **Respiratory depression:** Risk increases during initiation, dose escalation, or when combined with benzodiazepines, alcohol, antihistamines, or other CNS depressants.
- **Sedation and psychomotor impairment:** Counsel patients to avoid driving or operating machinery until individual response is known.
- **Constipation, nausea, pruritus:** Common; consider prophylactic bowel regimen and symptomatic treatments.
- **Dependence and misuse:** Prolonged use can cause tolerance and physical dependence; monitor for opioid use disorder and diversion.
- **Serotonin toxicity:** Rare but possible when combined with serotonergic antidepressants or MAOIs.

## Drug Interactions and Contraindications
- Strong CYP2D6 inhibitors (e.g., fluoxetine, paroxetine, bupropion, quinidine) can blunt analgesia by reducing morphine formation.
- CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., clarithromycin, azoles, some antivirals) may shift metabolism toward morphine, increasing toxicity risk; inducers can reduce effect.
- Contraindicated in significant respiratory depression, acute asthma without resuscitation capability, or paralytic ileus.
- Avoid postpartum use while breastfeeding if mother is an ultra-rapid metabolizer; monitor infants for excessive sleepiness or limpness if exposure occurs.

## Tapering and Discontinuation
- For therapy beyond several days, taper gradually (e.g., 10% dose reduction every 3–7 days) to minimize withdrawal symptoms such as rhinorrhea, anxiety, abdominal cramps, and insomnia.
- Pause or slow taper if severe withdrawal or uncontrolled pain emerges; consider non-opioid adjuncts and supportive therapies.

## Harm Reduction and Patient Education
- Provide clear instructions on maximum daily doses and avoiding duplicate acetaminophen or NSAID exposure from combination products.
- Encourage safe storage away from children and others; dispose of unused tablets through take-back programs when available.
- Offer naloxone and overdose education for patients at elevated risk or when co-prescribing sedatives.
