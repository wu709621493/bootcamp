# Homo sapiens biology: starvation to starved death

## Scope and caution
This depiction summarizes how the human body responds to prolonged energy deprivation, from early fasting to terminal starvation. It is educational and medical in tone, avoids romanticization, and emphasizes variability based on age, baseline health, hydration, temperature, infection, and access to care.

## Phase 1: Early fasting (hours to ~2 days)
- **Fuel shift:** Blood glucose falls; glycogen in liver and muscle is broken down to maintain glucose for the brain and red blood cells.
- **Hormonal response:** Insulin drops; glucagon, epinephrine, and cortisol rise, increasing glucose production and fat mobilization.
- **Symptoms:** Hunger, irritability, lightheadedness, fatigue; mild dehydration if intake is reduced.
- **Physiology:** Basal metabolic rate begins to decline; ketone production starts to rise.

## Phase 2: Ketotic adaptation (~2–14 days)
- **Fuel shift:** Lipolysis accelerates; fatty acids and ketone bodies become dominant fuels. The brain adapts to ketones, reducing glucose needs.
- **Protein sparing (partial):** Muscle breakdown slows compared to the first days, but continues to supply essential amino acids for gluconeogenesis.
- **Electrolytes:** Sodium, potassium, phosphate, and magnesium can drift low, especially with vomiting/diarrhea or diuretic effects.
- **Symptoms:** Weight loss, cold intolerance, weakness, orthostatic dizziness, reduced libido, slowed cognition.

## Phase 3: Prolonged starvation (weeks)
- **Energy conservation:** Resting heart rate and blood pressure drop; body temperature decreases; movement becomes minimal to conserve energy.
- **Organ effects:** Cardiac muscle mass decreases; immunity weakens; wound healing slows; hair and skin become dry and fragile.
- **Endocrine changes:** Thyroid hormone activity decreases; reproductive axis is suppressed (amenorrhea in many females; reduced testosterone in males).
- **Neuropsych:** Apathy, depression, impaired attention, irritability, and reduced pain tolerance can occur.

## Phase 4: Advanced starvation (weeks to months)
- **Protein depletion:** As fat stores shrink, the body increasingly catabolizes muscle and organ protein, including the diaphragm and heart.
- **Edema:** Low albumin and electrolyte disturbances can cause swelling in the legs, abdomen, or face.
- **Infection risk:** Severe immunosuppression increases susceptibility to pneumonia, sepsis, and skin infections.
- **Functional collapse:** Marked weakness, inability to stand or maintain body temperature, confusion, and delirium may appear.

## Phase 5: Terminal starvation and starved death
- **Multi-organ failure:** Progressive cardiac failure, arrhythmias, and respiratory muscle fatigue lead to insufficient circulation and oxygenation.
- **Hypoglycemia and electrolyte collapse:** Critically low glucose and electrolyte derangements (especially potassium and phosphate) can trigger fatal arrhythmias or seizures.
- **CNS decline:** Reduced cerebral perfusion causes stupor, coma, and loss of protective reflexes.
- **Final pathway:** Death typically results from cardiac arrest, respiratory failure, overwhelming infection, or complications of electrolyte collapse.

## Modifiers and risks that accelerate decline
- **Dehydration:** Often shortens survival dramatically and increases electrolyte instability.
- **Illness or injury:** Infections, burns, or wounds raise metabolic demand and hasten protein loss.
- **Temperature extremes:** Cold increases energy demand and hypothermia risk; heat increases dehydration.
- **Age and body composition:** Infants, older adults, and individuals with low baseline fat or chronic disease decline faster.

## Clinical considerations (summary)
- **Refeeding syndrome risk:** After prolonged starvation, rapid reintroduction of calories can cause dangerous electrolyte shifts; refeeding must be gradual with monitoring.
- **Medical care priorities:** Hydration, electrolyte correction, controlled nutritional support, infection surveillance, and warming.

## Ethical note
Starvation is a preventable cause of death. This depiction is meant to inform humanitarian, clinical, and educational efforts, not to normalize or minimize suffering.
