# Thymus

The thymus is a primary lymphoid organ situated in the anterior mediastinum, superior to the heart and posterior to the sternum. It is most active during childhood and adolescence, gradually involuting with age as functional tissue is replaced by fat.

## Structure and development

- **Lobular organization:** The thymus is divided into lobules composed of a peripheral cortex rich in developing T cells and a central medulla containing more mature thymocytes and specialized epithelial cells.
- **Blood supply and barrier:** Arterial branches from the internal thoracic and inferior thyroid arteries supply the organ, while venous blood drains via the left brachiocephalic, inferior thyroid, and internal thoracic veins. A blood–thymus barrier in the cortex limits antigen exposure during T-cell maturation.
- **Ontogeny:** Thymic epithelial cells originate from the third pharyngeal pouch endoderm, with hematopoietic progenitors seeding the organ from the bone marrow. Involution accelerates after puberty but residual thymic activity persists into adulthood.

## Immune function

- **T-cell maturation:** Early thymocytes undergo sequential stages defined by CD4 and CD8 expression, rearranging T-cell receptor genes and undergoing selection.
  - **Positive selection** in the cortex preserves thymocytes that recognize self-major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules.
  - **Negative selection** in the medulla removes thymocytes that bind self-antigens too strongly, aided by medullary thymic epithelial cells expressing the AIRE transcription factor to present tissue-restricted antigens.
- **T-cell repertoire:** The thymus generates naive, self-tolerant T cells including conventional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and regulatory T cells essential for peripheral tolerance.

## Clinical considerations

- **Congenital disorders:** DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion) can cause thymic hypoplasia or aplasia, leading to T-cell immunodeficiency and hypocalcemia from parathyroid agenesis.
- **Autoimmunity:** AIRE mutations result in autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1 due to defective central tolerance. Thymic abnormalities may contribute to other autoimmune conditions such as myasthenia gravis.
- **Neoplasms:** Thymomas arise from epithelial cells and often present with paraneoplastic syndromes like myasthenia gravis or pure red cell aplasia. Thymic carcinomas are rarer and more aggressive.
- **Transplantation and regeneration:** Thymic tissue or thymic-derived factors are being investigated to reconstitute T-cell immunity after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation or in age-related immune decline.

## Key points for learners

- The thymus is essential for establishing a diverse, self-tolerant T-cell repertoire.
- Cortical positive selection ensures MHC restriction; medullary negative selection enforces self-tolerance via AIRE-dependent antigen presentation.
- Thymic size and output decline with age, but residual function remains clinically relevant.
- Congenital absence, autoimmune dysregulation, and neoplastic transformation of the thymus have distinct clinical manifestations.
