# English and Chinese Terms for Trees

This note captures how English and Chinese encode ideas about trees differently in speech and writing.

## Mouth Shapes and English Tree Words
- English tree-related words such as **tree**, **leaf**, **branch**, and **root** require open-front vowels and sustained airflow, mirroring the sense of growth and extension.
- The plosive **tr** onset in *tree* gives an initial burst similar to a sprout breaking the soil, while the long vowel and fricatives in *leaf* and *branch* evoke lingering motion.
- These mouth shapes and airflow patterns contribute to an embodied sense of upward or outward growth when speaking the words.

## Graphical Abstraction in Chinese Characters
- Chinese tree-related characters often derive from the pictograph **木** (tree) and extend it with radicals and added strokes to convey function or relationship.
- Examples:
  - **林** and **森** duplicate **木** to indicate a grove or forest, visually multiplying trunks to show density.
  - **根** combines **木** with **艮**, tying the idea of a tree to firmness and anchoring for "root."
  - **枝** adds the **支** component to suggest branching support.
- The graphic design emphasizes spatial structure and semantic context more than the mouth feel of pronunciation, foregrounding visual abstraction of growth and composition.

## Bridging the Two Perspectives
- English leans on phonetic embodiment—how the mouth moves—while Chinese leans on visual morphology—how strokes combine to signify structure.
- Considering both perspectives highlights complementary ways humans map physical experience (growth, branching, rooting) into language: through the kinetic act of speech and the visual logic of characters.
