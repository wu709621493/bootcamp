# “Cure the Beef!” — A Scientific Game to Approach Prion Cure

## One-line pitch
A cooperative strategy game where players act as interdisciplinary research teams racing to discover, validate, and responsibly deploy interventions that reduce prion infectivity without causing ecological or clinical harm.

## Why this game exists
Prion diseases are unusual because the infectious agent is a misfolded protein, not a virus or bacterium. That makes intuitive thinking hard for students and non-specialists. This game converts real scientific constraints—protein folding, assay uncertainty, biosafety, translational bottlenecks, and ethics—into mechanics that are engaging but grounded.

## Learning goals
By the end of one game session, players should be able to:

1. Explain why prion diseases are hard to treat.
2. Distinguish between **in vitro signal reduction** and **actual infectivity reduction**.
3. Understand why replication, controls, and statistical power matter.
4. Balance speed, rigor, funding, and public trust.
5. Recognize that “cure” claims require staged evidence from bench to clinic.

## Audience and playtime
- Audience: high-school advanced biology, undergraduate life science, public science clubs.
- Players: 2–6.
- Session length: 45–90 minutes.
- Mode: cooperative with optional competitive leaderboard mode.

## Core game loop
Each round represents one research quarter:

1. **Plan**: choose projects (screen compounds, improve assays, animal model study, structural simulation, communication campaign).
2. **Allocate**: divide budget, personnel, and biosafety capacity.
3. **Execute**: roll/resolve outcome cards with uncertainty modifiers.
4. **Review**: peer review phase checks reproducibility and controls.
5. **Publish/Translate**: move promising candidates to next evidence tier.
6. **Event**: random event card (funding cuts, contamination risk, media hype, policy shifts).

Players win if they achieve a validated intervention profile before outbreak pressure reaches a critical threshold.

## Scientific mechanics (mapped to real concepts)

### 1) Misfolding dynamics
- Board tracks healthy protein pool vs misfolded seed burden.
- Some actions reduce signal but not seed burden (teaches false optimism).

### 2) Evidence tiers
Candidate interventions move through tiers:
- Tier 0: in silico hypothesis
- Tier 1: biochemical assay
- Tier 2: cell model
- Tier 3: animal model
- Tier 4: translational readiness

Skipping tiers increases failure risk and trust penalties.

### 3) Reproducibility meter
Results from single labs are fragile. Multi-site replication boosts confidence score.
Low confidence can trigger retractions in event phases.

### 4) Biosafety and ethics track
Risky shortcuts can accelerate progress but raise bioethics incidents. Too many incidents end the game regardless of scientific progress.

### 5) Public trust index
Overclaiming “breakthroughs” without robust evidence reduces trust. Low trust can freeze funding and trial enrollment.

## Roles (asymmetric strengths)
- **Protein Biophysicist**: strong in structure/mechanism cards.
- **Medicinal Chemist**: boosts optimization and toxicity mitigation.
- **Neuropathologist**: improves model interpretation.
- **Biostatistician**: reduces false-positive outcomes.
- **Clinical Trial Lead**: speeds translation under strict criteria.
- **Science Communicator**: stabilizes trust during uncertainty.

## Components
- Main board (disease pressure, trust, biosafety, funding).
- Candidate cards (small molecules, biologics, chaperone strategies, clearance enhancers).
- Assay cards (RT-QuIC-like signal, infectivity bioassay analog, structural readout).
- Event deck (media cycles, regulation, lab incidents, grant climates).
- Ethics deck (informed consent issues, animal welfare constraints, dual-use review).
- Dice or digital probability app for uncertainty resolution.

## Example turn (short)
- Team selects “Compound Library Screen” + “Orthogonal Validation Assay.”
- Initial screen yields a strong hit.
- Biostatistician catches low-n artifact; team repeats with larger sample.
- Effect shrinks but remains significant.
- Team avoids overhyping, preserving trust.
- Next round opens Tier 2 testing with moderate confidence.

## Scoring and end states
### Cooperative victory
Achieve all of the following before crisis threshold:
- Candidate reaches Tier 4.
- Confidence score above validation cutoff.
- Biosafety incidents below max.
- Public trust above minimum.

### Failure states
- Outbreak pressure reaches maximum.
- Biosafety/ethics breach cap exceeded.
- Trust collapse blocks translation.

## Classroom / workshop use
- Pre-game: 10-minute primer on protein folding and prions.
- Mid-game pause: discuss one “false positive” and one “ethical tradeoff.”
- Post-game debrief: compare player path vs real translational pipelines.

## Optional digital mode
A companion notebook/app can:
- simulate parameter sweeps,
- visualize confidence intervals,
- let players test policy choices (e.g., strict regulation vs emergency acceleration),
- export a “lab report” at game end.

## Design principles for scientific integrity
1. Never present speculative effects as proven cures.
2. Reward rigorous methods more than lucky outcomes.
3. Make ethical and social constraints first-class mechanics.
4. Keep language precise: “candidate,” “signal,” “evidence,” “validation.”

## Quick prototype recipe
1. Print a one-page board with four tracks: pressure, trust, safety, funding.
2. Create 30 candidate cards and 30 event cards.
3. Use a d6 with modifiers for role strengths and method quality.
4. Run three pilot sessions and tune for:
   - too-easy wins (raise replication requirements),
   - frustration spikes (add recovery actions),
   - misconception hotspots (improve debrief prompts).

## Closing note
“Cure the Beef!” works best when framed not as “gamifying disease,” but as practicing the discipline of responsible discovery under uncertainty. The objective is scientific literacy and better reasoning about what a real cure would require.
