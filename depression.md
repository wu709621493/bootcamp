# Depression: Overview and Practical Notes

Depression is a mood disorder that can affect how a person feels, thinks, and functions. It is distinct from short-term sadness because symptoms often persist for at least two weeks and interfere with daily activities.

## Common symptoms
- Persistent low mood or loss of interest in activities
- Fatigue or low energy
- Sleep changes (insomnia or oversleeping)
- Appetite changes or weight fluctuation
- Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
- Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt
- Physical complaints such as headaches or digestive issues
- Thoughts of self-harm or suicide

## Risk factors and contributors
- Personal or family history of depression or other mood disorders
- Major life stressors (loss, trauma, discrimination, chronic illness)
- Certain medications or substance use
- Hormonal shifts (postpartum, thyroid issues)
- Social isolation or lack of support

## Screening and diagnosis
- Standardized tools like the PHQ-9 or GAD-7 can flag symptoms but do not replace a clinical evaluation.
- A healthcare professional assesses symptom duration, severity, and impact on functioning, and screens for other medical causes.

## Evidence-supported treatments
- **Psychotherapy:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), interpersonal therapy (IPT), and behavioral activation are common first-line options.
- **Medication:** Antidepressants (such as SSRIs or SNRIs) can be effective, especially for moderate to severe cases; dosing and duration should be guided by a clinician.
- **Lifestyle supports:** Regular sleep, balanced nutrition, physical activity, and reducing substance use can complement formal treatment.
- **Combined care:** Therapy plus medication often provides stronger and more durable relief than either alone for many people.

## Self-management and coping strategies
- Keep a consistent sleep-wake routine and limit screen time close to bedtime.
- Break tasks into small steps and celebrate completing each step.
- Schedule activities that bring even mild enjoyment or a sense of accomplishment.
- Maintain social contact through check-ins, support groups, or community activities.
- Track mood, sleep, and triggers to share with a healthcare provider.
- Reduce alcohol and non-prescribed drug use, which can worsen mood symptoms.

## When to seek immediate help
- If you experience thoughts of self-harm or suicide, contact a local emergency number, crisis hotline, or go to the nearest emergency room.
- Reach out to a trusted person and avoid being alone during acute crises.

## Supporting someone with depression
- Listen without judgment and avoid minimizing their feelings.
- Offer practical help with daily tasks and encourage professional support.
- Check in regularly; consistent contact can reduce isolation.
- Learn warning signs of crisis (talk of death, giving away possessions, sudden calm after despair) and connect them with urgent help if needed.

## Notes on safety and follow-up
- Never stop psychiatric medications abruptly without medical guidance; sudden changes can cause withdrawal or relapse.
- Treatment plans often require adjustments over weeks to months. Regular follow-up allows fine-tuning and monitoring side effects.
- Recovery is rarely linear. Relapses can happen; seeking help early can prevent symptoms from deepening.
