# Surplus

Surplus is the amount of resources or value that remains after essential needs or obligations are met. It shows up in economics, ecology, logistics, and personal productivity, where the extra capacity can either be reinvested or wasted.

## Economic surplus

- **Consumer surplus:** The difference between what a buyer is willing to pay and the lower price they actually pay. It grows when prices fall or when products deliver more perceived value.
- **Producer surplus:** The difference between the price received and the minimum price at which a seller would have been willing to supply the good. It increases with higher market prices, cost reductions, or productivity gains.
- **Deadweight loss:** Surplus that disappears because of market inefficiencies, like overproduction from subsidies or underproduction from price ceilings. Reducing frictions, improving competition, and pricing externalities can recover some of this lost value.

## Operational surplus

- **Inventory buffers:** Holding a small surplus of materials can protect against supply volatility, but excessive stock ties up capital and risks obsolescence.
- **Time buffers:** Extra time in schedules reduces failure risk but can invite procrastination. Well-designed buffers are transparent and regularly recalibrated.
- **Energy surplus:** Teams perform better when people have surplus cognitive and emotional energy. Practices such as manageable workloads, predictable schedules, and clear priorities help preserve this surplus.

## Surplus as resilience capital

Surplus is a form of resilience capital: it absorbs shocks and creates optionality. The challenge is to size and deploy surplus intentionally—large enough to handle variability, small enough to avoid waste. Continuous measurement, feedback loops, and deliberate reinvestment turn surplus into sustained advantage.
